\chapter{Linearization and Discretization}

\section{Linearizing Continuous-Time Models}

To obtain linear state-space representations of the Modified Four Tank System, the nonlinear dynamics and output relations are linearized around a stationary operating point $(x_s, u_s, d_s)$. Given the nominal steady-state pump flow rates $u_s = [F_{1s}, F_{2s}]^T$ and nominal disturbance flow rates $d_s = [F_{3s}, F_{4s}]^T$, the steady-state liquid masses $x_s = [m_{1s}, m_{2s}, m_{3s}, m_{4s}]^T$, measurements $y_s$, and controlled outputs $z_s$ satisfy the equilibrium conditions:
\begin{align}
    0 &= f(x_s, u_s, d_s, p) \\
    y_s &= g(x_s, p) \\
    z_s &= h(x_s, p)
\end{align}
Deviation variables describing perturbations around this equilibrium are defined as:
\begin{align}
    X(t) &= x(t) - x_s, & U(t) &= u(t) - u_s, & D(t) &= d(t) - d_s, \\
    Y(t) &= y(t) - y_s, & Z(t) &= z(t) - z_s
\end{align}

\subsection{Deterministic Model}

In the deterministic formulation, the nonlinearity arises from Bernoulli's law in the gravity-driven tank outflows, $q_i = a_i \sqrt{2 g h_i}$. This nonlinear height term is approximated by taking a first-order Taylor series expansion of $f(x(t), u(t), d(t), p)$, $g(x(t), p)$, and $h(x(t), p)$ around the steady-state operating point $(x_s, u_s, d_s)$:
\begin{align}
    \dot{x}(t) &\approx f(x_s, u_s, d_s, p) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x_s, u_s, d_s, p)(x(t) - x_s) \nonumber \\
    &\quad + \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}(x_s, u_s, d_s, p)(u(t) - u_s) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial d}(x_s, u_s, d_s, p)(d(t) - d_s)
\end{align}
Since $\dot{x}_s = f(x_s, u_s, d_s, p) = 0$, the linearized continuous-time deterministic state-space model in deviation variables becomes:
\begin{align}
    \dot{X}(t) &= A X(t) + B U(t) + G_d D(t) \\
    Y(t) &= C X(t) \\
    Z(t) &= C_z X(t)
\end{align}
Evaluating the partial derivatives of the mass balances with respect to the states $m_i$ yields the tank time constants $T_i$:
\begin{equation}
    T_i = \frac{A_i}{a_i} \sqrt{\frac{2 h_{is}}{g}}, \quad \text{with} \quad h_{is} = \frac{m_{is}}{\rho A_i}, \quad i \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}
\end{equation}
The resulting system matrices $(A, B, G_d, C, C_z)$ are given by the Jacobians:
\begin{align}
    A &= \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x_s, u_s, d_s, p) = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -\frac{1}{T_1} & 0 & \frac{1}{T_3} & 0 \\
        0 & -\frac{1}{T_2} & 0 & \frac{1}{T_4} \\
        0 & 0 & -\frac{1}{T_3} & 0 \\
        0 & 0 & 0 & -\frac{1}{T_4}
    \end{bmatrix} \\
    B &= \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}(x_s, u_s, d_s, p) = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \rho \gamma_1 & 0 \\
        0 & \rho \gamma_2 \\
        0 & \rho(1 - \gamma_2) \\
        \rho(1 - \gamma_1) & 0
    \end{bmatrix}, \quad
    G_d = \frac{\partial f}{\partial d}(x_s, u_s, d_s, p) = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0 & 0 \\
        0 & 0 \\
        \rho & 0 \\
        0 & \rho
    \end{bmatrix} \\
    C &= \frac{\partial g}{\partial x}(x_s, p) = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \frac{1}{\rho A_1} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
        0 & \frac{1}{\rho A_2} & 0 & 0
    \end{bmatrix}, \quad
    C_z = \frac{\partial h}{\partial x}(x_s, p) = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \frac{1}{\rho A_1} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
        0 & \frac{1}{\rho A_2} & 0 & 0
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

\subsection{Stochastic Nonlinear Model (ZOH)}

In the piecewise-constant stochastic model, the unmeasured disturbances $F_3$ and $F_4$ are modeled as random variables held constant over each sampling interval, $d(t) = d_k$ for $t_k \le t < t_{k+1}$, while sensor measurements are corrupted by additive Gaussian noise $v(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_{vv}(p))$. 

Because $d(t)$ is constant within each open sampling interval $(t_k, t_{k+1})$, the state trajectory remains continuously differentiable between sampling instants, and standard ordinary differential equation calculus applies. Decomposing the disturbance into its nominal mean $d_s = \mathbb{E}\{d_k\}$ and a zero-mean piecewise-constant random deviation $D_k = d_k - d_s \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q_d)$ yields the linearized continuous-time model:
\begin{align}
    \dot{X}(t) &= A X(t) + B U(t) + G_d D(t), \quad D(t) = D_k \text{ for } t_k \le t < t_{k+1} \\
    Y(t) &= C X(t) + v(t), \quad v(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_{vv}(p)) \\
    Z(t) &= C_z X(t)
\end{align}
where the Jacobian matrices $(A, B, G_d, C, C_z)$ are identical to those derived for the deterministic model, and the additive measurement noise $v(t)$ enters the linearized sensor equation directly without modification.

\subsection{Stochastic Nonlinear Model (SDE)}

In the stochastic differential equation (SDE) formulation, continuous-time process variations in the unmeasured inflows $F_3$ and $F_4$ are driven by a standard Wiener process $\omega(t)$ with incremental covariance $\mathbb{E}\{d\omega(t)d\omega(t)^T\} = I \, dt$:
\begin{equation}
    dx(t) = f(x(t), u(t), d(t), p) \, dt + \sigma(x(t), u(t), d(t), p) \, d\omega(t)
\end{equation}
Because the Wiener process $\omega(t)$ is nowhere differentiable, the derivative $\frac{d\omega(t)}{dt}$ is not well-defined in standard calculus, and the system must be linearized in It\^o differential form rather than time-derivative form. 

A first-order Taylor expansion is applied to the deterministic drift term $f(x(t), u(t), d(t), p) \, dt$ around the nominal operating point $(x_s, u_s, d_s)$, while the diffusion matrix $\sigma(x(t), u(t), d(t), p)$ is evaluated at the stationary equilibrium to obtain a constant matrix $G_\omega = \sigma(x_s, u_s, d_s, p)$. Since the stochastic disturbances enter the mass balances of Tank 3 and Tank 4 linearly, the diffusion term is state-independent, meaning $G_\omega = G_d \sigma_d$ (where $\sigma_d$ scales the intensity of the Wiener process) is exact. The resulting linearized continuous-time SDE system is:
\begin{align}
    dX(t) &= \left( A X(t) + B U(t) + G_d D(t) \right) dt + G_\omega \, d\omega(t) \\
    Y(t) &= C X(t) + v(t), \quad v(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R_{vv}(p)) \\
    Z(t) &= C_z X(t)
\end{align}
If the mean disturbance level is constant at $d(t) = d_s$ and all stochastic variations around $d_s$ are captured by the diffusion term $G_\omega \, d\omega(t)$, the deterministic disturbance deviation vanishes ($D(t) = 0$), simplifying the drift term to $(A X(t) + B U(t)) \, dt$.

\section{Deriving Transfer Functions}

\subsection{Deterministic Model}

For the deterministic continuous-time linear model, the relation between the system inputs and outputs in the frequency domain is established by combining the state-space matrices into an augmented input matrix. Stating the manipulated input matrix $B$ and the disturbance matrix $G_d$ as a combined block matrix $B^* = \begin{bmatrix} B & G_d \end{bmatrix}$, the state-space equations in deviation variables are expressed as:
\begin{align}
    \dot{X}(t) &= A X(t) + B^* U^*(t) \\
    Y(t) &= C X(t)
\end{align}
where $U^*(t) = \begin{bmatrix} U(t)^T & D(t)^T \end{bmatrix}^T$. Applying the Laplace transform under zero initial conditions yields the standard multivariable transfer function matrix:
\begin{equation}
    H^*(s) = C(sI - A)^{-1} B^* = \begin{bmatrix} C(sI - A)^{-1} B & C(sI - A)^{-1} G_d \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
This formulation simultaneously provides the input-to-output transfer functions and the disturbance-to-output response models.

\subsection{Zero-Order Hold Model}

The derivation for the zero-order hold (ZOH) formulation follows a similar analytical pathway in discrete time. Given a constant sampling period $T_s$ and inputs held constant over each sampling interval via zero-order hold electronics, the continuous-time linear system is discretized into the state-space representation:
\begin{align}
    X_{k+1} &= \bar{A} X_k + \bar{B}^* U^*_k \\
    Y_k &= C X_k
\end{align}
where the discrete-time matrices $\bar{A}$ and $\bar{B}^* = \begin{bmatrix} \bar{B} & \bar{G}_d \end{bmatrix}$ are computed using the matrix exponential via conversion algorithms. Taking the Z transform of the discrete state-space equations yields the discrete transfer function matrix:
\begin{equation}
    H^*(z) = C(zI - \bar{A})^{-1} \bar{B}^*
\end{equation}
This describes the exact sampled-data frequency response corresponding to the continuous ZOH process.

\subsection{Stochastic Differential Equation formulation}

When the unmeasured disturbances are modeled via stochastic differential equations driven by a Wiener process, the classical deterministic Laplace transform is replaced by a stochastic formulation utilizing Itô integrals. Applying the stochastic chain rule (Itô's lemma) to the product of the deterministic exponential term $e^{-st}$ and the state vector $X_t$, we expand the differential of the product:
\begin{equation}
    d\left(e^{-st} X_t\right) = -s e^{-st} X_t \, dt + e^{-st} dX_t
\end{equation}
Integrating this expression from time zero to infinity and taking the limit yields the stochastic integration-by-parts rule. Substituting the linear SDE formulation $dX_t = (AX_t + BU_t)dt + G_\omega dW_t$ into the integrated expression results in:
\begin{equation}
    (sI - A) X(s) = X_0 + B U(s) + G_\omega \mathcal{W}(s)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{W}(s) = \int_0^\infty e^{-st} dW_t$ represents the Laplace-transformed Wiener integral. Solving for $X(s)$ and mapping to the outputs provides the frequency-domain representation incorporating both deterministic inputs and the shape-filtered stochastic noise processes.

\section{Comparison of Gains and Time Constants}
\label{sec:p5-comparison}

The analytical transfer functions are compared with the continuous-time
models identified in Problem~4, given in \eqref{eq:p4-identified}.
Both models map pump-flow deviations to lower-tank level deviations, so
their gains have units of $\mathrm{cm/(cm^3/s)}$ and their time constants
are in seconds. To make the comparison consistent, the analytical model
is evaluated at the initial operating point of the identification
experiments,
\begin{equation}
 u_s=[300,300]^T\ \mathrm{cm^3/s},\qquad
 d_s=[20,20]^T\ \mathrm{cm^3/s}.
\end{equation}
This differs from the operating points used for controller design in
Problem~3. The corresponding equilibrium outlet flows are
$q_s=[290,350,110,140]^T\ \mathrm{cm^3/s}$, giving
\begin{equation}
 [T_1,T_2,T_3,T_4]^T
 =[74.6163,90.0541,28.3027,36.0216]^T\ \mathrm{s}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Analytical Input--Output Model}

Using the matrices derived in Section~5.1, the pump-to-level transfer
matrix is
\begin{equation}
 G(s)=C(sI-A)^{-1}B
 =\begin{bmatrix}
 \dfrac{\gamma_1T_1/A_1}{1+T_1s}&
 \dfrac{(1-\gamma_2)T_1/A_1}{(1+T_1s)(1+T_3s)}\\[6pt]
 \dfrac{(1-\gamma_1)T_2/A_2}{(1+T_2s)(1+T_4s)}&
 \dfrac{\gamma_2T_2/A_2}{1+T_2s}
 \end{bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:p5-analytical-structure}
\end{equation}
The density cancels when mass states are converted to level outputs.
With $\gamma_1=0.6$, $\gamma_2=0.7$, and
$A_i=380.1327\ \mathrm{cm^2}$, this becomes
\begin{equation}
 G(s)=\begin{bmatrix}
 \dfrac{0.117774}{1+74.6163s}&
 \dfrac{0.0588870}{(1+28.3027s)(1+74.6163s)}\\[6pt]
 \dfrac{0.0947607}{(1+36.0216s)(1+90.0541s)}&
 \dfrac{0.165831}{1+90.0541s}
 \end{bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:p5-analytical}
\end{equation}
Rows correspond to $h_1,h_2$ and columns to $F_1,F_2$. The direct
channels have one pole, whereas the cross channels have two poles
because their flow passes through an upper tank before reaching a lower
tank. The identified models use these same channel orders; agreement
in order therefore follows from the chosen fitting structure.

\subsection{Static Gains}

For each channel, the static gain is $K_{ij}=G_{ij}(0)$.
Table~\ref{tab:p5-gains} compares the local analytical gains with the

\end{equation}
The table also gives the exact equilibrium gains for the noiseless
25\% steps, calculated from \eqref{eq:p4-secant} with
$\Delta F_j=75\ \mathrm{cm^3/s}$.

All identified gains exceed the local analytical values, with differences
of approximately 2--8\%. The main systematic cause is the finite positive
step: the analytical gain is a tangent at the initial equilibrium,
whereas the step experiment measures a secant across a change in level.
Since $h_i=q_i^2/(2ga_i^2)$, the finite-step gain contains the positive
correction
\begin{equation}
 K_{ij}^{\mathrm{step}}-K_{ij}
 =\frac{\beta_{ij}^2\Delta F_j}{2ga_i^2},\qquad
 [\beta_{ij}]=\begin{bmatrix}0.6&0.3\\0.4&0.7\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The identified gains are within 1.91\% of these exact 25\% equilibrium
gains. The remaining differences reflect fitting a transient record
affected by process noise, measurement noise, and baseline estimation.
Thus the gain discrepancy is consistent with approximating a nonlinear
finite-step response by a local linear model.

\subsection{Time Constants}

For a real stable pole $p$, the corresponding time constant is $-1/p$.
Table~\ref{tab:p5-time-constants} compares the analytical pole time
constants with those identified from the noisy 25\% steps. For each
cross channel, both pairs are sorted from the smaller to the larger
time constant, and relative differences are calculated as
$100(\widehat T/T-1)\%$. This pairs the faster and slower modes for
comparison; it does not uniquely assign fitted poles to physical tanks.
\begin{table}[htbp]
 \centering
 \caption{Pole time constants and signed relative differences. Cross-channel
 pairs are ordered from faster to slower mode.}
 \label{tab:p5-time-constants}
 \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
 \hline
 Channel & Analytical (s) & Identified (s) & Difference (\%)\\
 \hline
 $F_1\to h_1$ & $74.62$ & $83.91$ & $+12.46$\\
 $F_2\to h_1$ & $(28.30,74.62)$ & $(37.36,76.09)$ & $(+32.00,+1.98)$\\
 $F_1\to h_2$ & $(36.02,90.05)$ & $(32.63,109.74)$ & $(-9.41,+21.85)$\\
 $F_2\to h_2$ & $90.05$ & $101.14$ & $+12.31$\\
 \hline
 \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The direct-channel time constants are about 12\% larger than their
analytical values. This is consistent with the slower dynamics at higher
levels: the outlet sensitivity $dq_i/dh_i=a_i\sqrt{g/(2h_i)}$ decreases
as the level rises, while
$T_i=(A_i/a_i)\sqrt{2h_i/g}$ increases. A single time constant fitted
over the whole step is therefore an effective approximation to dynamics
that change throughout the transient.

The cross-channel differences are less uniform. The slower modes differ
by about 2\% and 22\%, while the faster modes differ by $+32\%$ and
$-9\%$. Two-pole fits can trade off their time constants to approximate
the same noisy transient, so these differences cannot be attributed to
level dependence alone. The channels were fitted independently and need
not share a lower-tank pole: for example, the analytical $T_1$ occurs in
both $G_{11}$ and $G_{12}$, whereas their fitted slow time constants are
83.91 and 76.09 seconds. Without repeated identification experiments or
parameter confidence intervals, the significance of individual pole
differences cannot be established.

The comparison supports the analytical model as a local description of
the pump-to-level dynamics. The identified model approximates the noisy
25\% excursion more closely, with larger gains and generally slower
dominant dynamics. For smaller steps, the deterministic finite-step
gains approach the analytical gains, although weaker excitation makes
identification more sensitive to noise.
